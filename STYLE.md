# Writing style

This guide sets the prose style for the course. It follows the classic
style described by Francis-Noël Thomas and Mark Turner in *Clear and Simple
as the Truth: Writing Classic Prose*, and defended again by Steven Pinker
in *The Sense of Style*.

## Frame of mind

- The reader is an equal learner, not an audience to persuade.
- The motive is truth: show the idea clearly, as it is.
- The prose educates; it never pitches.

## Rules

- Use active voice. Name who does what.
- Prefer plain words. Choose the everyday word over the technical one
  when both say the same.
- Use concrete nouns and verbs. Favor "sends data" over "enacts a data
  transfer".
- Keep one idea moving at a time. Break a long sentence into two.
- Cut. Delete a word when the sentence stays clear without it.
- Never use an em dash. Use a comma, a colon, or a period instead.
- No hype, slogans, or rhetorical fire. Write like a patient teacher.
- Write complete declarative sentences. Do not use fragments for effect.
- State the fact; do not command the reader to watch, notice, or look.
- Plain verbs: changed, replaced, moved, answered. Do not dramatize.
- Keep genuine questions and the word "you". They teach. Slogans do not.

## Sentence rhythm

- Aim for a default near 25 words per sentence.
- Allow a ceiling near 40 words and a floor near five.
- Vary the lengths. Two short sentences after one long one give the
  page a natural rhythm.
- Sentence length is guidance, not law. Clarity always wins.

## Teaching method

- Teach by doing first. Let the learner act before anyone names the
  idea.
- Start concrete, then fade to abstract. Show the terminal before the
  loop, and the shop before the contract.
- Add one "do it now" block per section. The action happens in the
  learner's own browser, never on a remote service.
- Add a recap pass after each interactive widget. Number the review
  points.
- Set the scope openly. Write the sentence "You do not need X yet"
  when a term can wait.
- Anticipate the learner's question. Answer it in a short aside.
- Expand every acronym at first use. AJAX becomes "AJAX, Asynchronous
  JavaScript and XML".
- Use "we" for shared actions and "you" for the learner's own actions.
- Keep present tense. The reader is in the room.
- Signal key terms with strong emphasis only when they carry the idea.
- Keep captions beside their figure or widget. Never separate them.
- Exclude seductive details. A detail outside the idea competes for
  attention. Mayer found that such details hurt learning.
- Use dry humor; ban hype. A memorable, plain rule teaches. A slogan
  does not.

## Why

- GOV.UK documents a 25-word sentence limit and cites accessibility
  reasoning: "when you write more, people understand less".
- Randomized controlled studies show plain language improves how well
  lay readers understand technical material.
- Orwell's six rules in "Politics and the English Language" say the same
  from the writer's side: prefer short words, cut every possible word,
  prefer the active voice, and break a rule rather than say something
  barbarous.
- Thomas and Turner argue style is the heart of prose, not an accessory.
  Zinsser adds warmth and humanity to the same discipline.
