Section 02

How the idea got lost

The loop you stepped through did not appear in 2020. Someone invented it in 1965, someone named it in 2000, and half of an industry forgot it by 2011. Watch who carries the next move, and where it lands.

Ted Nelson coined two words in 1965: hypertext, and the broader idea, hypermedia. Branching text. Media that join. Documents that point at other documents, forever.

In 1989 Tim Berners-Lee proposed that thought in a CERN document. Linked documents, read by a client nobody owned. Later, the hypertext carried both facts and controls, and that mix was the part that would get lost.

In 2000 Roy Fielding named that style in his doctoral dissertation: REST. You met his words in Section 01. Here is the part most copies missed: the controls must travel with the facts, or the architecture is not REST.

Now the detour. Enterprise software wants reliable conversations, so it freezes them into contracts. A schema describes every message; a stack generates the code. Servers answer with data, never with the next move.

In 2005 the shift happens inside a page, invisibly. AJAX lets a site update in place without a reload, and the word “API” replaces “server” in ordinary talk. Nobody votes to stop sending controls. Each request quietly chooses data instead.

You tap a native app, and it speaks an app’s language. You watch a screen animate where a full reload cannot follow. Teams that work separately want contracts they can test. Each reason holds on its own.

  • screens that animate in place

Fashion
  • answers that carry data, never the next move
  • a spec kept outside the document
  • clients that must be told again and shipped

The loss happens in how the word gets used. Fielding’s acronym, HATEOAS, shrinks to a JSON field of links. A link you must already know is a note, not a control. Strawman “REST” comes to mean facts alone, spelled with verbs.

Fielding watches and objects on the public record. In 2008 he writes the piece Section 01 quoted: REST APIs must be hypertext-driven. The name survives its author. The behavior does not.

2011 seals the bargain. Swagger standardizes the shape of the conversation, field by field. Renamed OpenAPI in 2016, it still gates the conversation today. Teams receive exact expectations and give up the open-ended loop. The document carrying controls cedes to the schema describing data.

One thread never died. Documentation sites, forums, and plain web apps kept the original loop running, decade after decade. A new wave of tooling, htmx and Hotwire among it, re-learned what the terminal always knew: send the document, let it carry the moves.
